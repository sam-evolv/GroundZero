# Product motion video design standard

Do not apply it blindly where the delivery format requires a plain founder recording, a long readable walkthrough or unembellished evidence capture.

## What the references demonstrate

### 1. Leo: trading flow as a tactile miniature narrative

The 16.5-second Fomo piece has a clear action arc:

- 0.0–2.5 s: dark product orientation, moving from compact instrument identity into a readable leveraged position card;
- 2.5–5.2 s: position controls appear and the primary choice is staged with blue and green actions;
- about 5.2 s: a circular aperture flips the visual world from dark UI to white parameter space;
- 5.2–7.5 s: market/limit selection and percentage controls are isolated at macro scale;
- 7.5–10.0 s: price and percentage details are shown on an almost-white field, with one value treated as the visual event;
- 10.0–11.7 s: the interface turns into a tactile, runway-like 3D control and a key press becomes the climax;
- 11.7–13.7 s: a compact trade-confirmation card resolves the action;
- 13.7–16.5 s: lowercase promise, “trade by your own rules,” then the Fomo wordmark.

Design lessons:

- Present one transaction, not a feature inventory.
- Let the product’s own nouns drive the sequence: position, choice, parameter, confirmation.
- Alternate readable product context with macro abstraction. Context explains; abstraction makes it feel tactile.
- Use a repeated brand geometry, here circles, pills and rounded cards, as the transition grammar.
- Reserve the largest depth and camera move for the decisive action, not every screen.
- End with a short promise that names the user benefit, then the mark. Do not introduce a new claim at the logo.

### 2. Leo: four-pane mobile interaction choreography

The 11.3-second mobile piece uses a persistent 2×2 grid rather than serial full-screen cuts:

- top left: dark trading position and result;
- top right: bright purchase/progress phone with a neon-green completion ring;
- bottom left: sky-backed messaging/phone interaction, later a floating green object;
- bottom right: dark social or feed interface.

The panes change at different moments while the overall grid remains stable. Black, white, sky blue and deep navy give each quadrant a distinct world; neon green ties them together. Some surfaces are straight 2D crops, while the phones use perspective, edge highlights, shadows and off-axis movement to imply 3D depth.

Design lessons:

- A stable split-screen can carry multiple rapid interactions without making the entire frame geometrically unstable.
- Give each pane a unique tonal world, then use one shared accent to make the montage feel authored.
- Offset the beats between panes so the eye is handed from quadrant to quadrant rather than hit everywhere at once.
- Keep the quadrant boundaries rigid while allowing content inside them to rotate, rise, loop and settle.
- Use this treatment for teasers, reels or portfolio energy. Do not use four simultaneous panes for a demo that must prove one exact workflow or let viewers read dense text.

### 3. Varcy: interface journey transformed into brand reveal

The 13.9-second Grok piece follows one continuous dark cinematic world:

- 0.0–2.7 s: Grok prompt bar, typed task and immediate command context;
- 2.7–4.2 s: “Analyzing Post…” becomes a tunnel-like transition, making the system’s work visible;
- 4.2–6.8 s: the answer card appears on an angled floating plane, with depth and a moving highlight;
- 6.8–8.0 s: capability icons and labels emerge as secondary objects;
- 8.0–10.0 s: multiple media/result planes form a spatial field;
- 10.0–11.8 s: an orbital light path and flare compress the product world into the Grok symbol;
- 11.8–13.9 s: the symbol resolves, then the Grok wordmark holds cleanly.

Design lessons:

- Build the brand reveal out of the product’s own geometry and motion, rather than cutting to an unrelated logo animation.
- Treat system processing as a visible narrative beat when it is part of the product promise.
- Move from literal UI to spatial metaphor in stages: prompt, process, answer, capability field, mark.
- Keep typography and UI restrained when depth, glow and camera motion are already carrying the emotion.
- Use glows as directional cues and hierarchy, not a universal decoration.

## Portfolio-wide principles to reuse

1. **One causal arc.** Every short video should answer: what did the user start, what changed, what completed, and why should they care?
2. **Product pixels first.** Real reachable product footage remains authoritative. Motion design may frame, isolate or transition it, but must not invent inaccessible behavior.
3. **One visual event per beat.** Give each beat one primary action and one dominant focal point.
4. **Geometry as grammar.** Derive masks, wipes, apertures, cards, paths and logo transitions from the product’s actual shapes.
5. **Readable context, then sensation.** Establish the interface at readable scale before macro crops, perspective moves or abstraction.
6. **Purposeful depth.** Use 3D perspective to mark hierarchy, causality or climax. Avoid constant camera drift and ornamental parallax.
7. **Continuity across cuts.** Match position, direction, color, scale or shape so transitions feel inevitable rather than decorative.
8. **Motion with weight.** Prefer anticipation, acceleration, overshoot and settle over linear keyframes. Small controls should feel lighter than decisive actions.
9. **Brand color with restraint.** One accent can connect otherwise different scenes. Do not make every surface glow.
10. **End on meaning.** Resolve the action, state one short benefit if needed, then hold the mark long enough to register.
11. **Sound is structural.** Design audio after visual timing is locked, but before final polish. Sound should explain state changes, weight and continuity.
12. **Evidence and hype remain separate.** Teaser energy is not proof. For investor, sales or acceptance demos, keep readable evidence-bearing scenes and human-control points intact.

## Sound-design grammar

The measured waveforms show dense, rhythmically regular beds with discrete peaks around visual events. The first and second references have very low loudness range, suggesting intentionally compressed, always-present energy; the Grok piece has more dynamic build and release around its logo climax.

For future cuts:

- use a quiet bed or tonal pulse to maintain continuity;
- place a short UI tick at direct manipulation;
- add a lower body impact only for consequential changes;
- use a directional whoosh across camera travel or wipes;
- pre-lap a riser just before a major reveal;
- add a brief tonal sweetener when the action resolves;
- give the logo its own sonic signature, then leave a short tail;
- align transients to the perceived moment of contact or state change, not mechanically to every edit;
- vary pitch, stereo position and weight according to object size and movement direction;
- preserve micro-pauses so impacts retain contrast;
- never copy the references’ over-ceiling master levels. Final delivery should decode cleanly, avoid clipping and be reviewed on the target platform.

## Likely production method, labelled as inference

The source files only identify Twitter’s remuxer, not the creator’s authoring application. The following is the most plausible reproducible workflow, not a confirmed tool list:

1. Storyboard beats and one transition rule in Figma or a similar design tool.
2. Capture the real interface at high resolution and, where possible, 60 fps. Export static UI elements as separate transparent layers only when truthful compositing requires isolation.
3. Build motion in After Effects or an equivalent node/timeline compositor using masks, track mattes, shape layers, eased keyframes, motion blur, 2.5D cameras and perspective transforms.
4. Use Blender or Cinema 4D only for genuinely volumetric elements such as macro hardware, emissive tunnels or orbital logo objects. The flat UI scenes do not require a full 3D pipeline.
5. Lock picture before detailed sound. Build the audio from licensed or original UI ticks, impacts, whooshes, risers and a restrained tonal bed.
6. Export a high-quality master, then make a platform-specific H.264/AAC delivery encode. Inspect the final encoded frames, waveform, true peak, aspect ratio and remote platform rendering.

## Default production brief for future Donworth Studio videos

- Objective: one sentence and one observable user outcome.
- Runtime: choose the shortest duration that preserves comprehension; do not target 11–16 seconds when the content needs longer.
- Beat sheet: hook, context, action, transformation, confirmation, brand close.
- Product truth: list real, reachable surfaces and any illustrative framing.
- Motion grammar: one dominant geometric motif, one camera rule, one accent color.
- Sound map: bed, direct-manipulation ticks, major impacts, transitions, resolve, logo sting.
- Readability: review at normal speed and at the actual delivery size.
- Final gate: full decode, sampled frames, black/freeze checks, loudness and true peak, privacy review, claim reconciliation and platform read-back.

## Do not over-apply

- Do not turn a founder video into a promo reel when the submission rules forbid product footage, music or slides.
- Do not use split-screen density where one exact workflow must be understood.
- Do not replace real product pixels with reconstructed UI.
- Do not let glow, camera motion or sound impacts imply that an unavailable feature worked.
- Do not treat the inferred After Effects/3D workflow as creator-confirmed provenance.
