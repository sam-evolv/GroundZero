# OpenHouse energy intelligence sketch

## One-line thesis
OpenHouse can become the intelligence layer for new homes and retrofit homes: it learns the home’s fabric, devices, usage patterns, and ownership context, then helps people use the house properly, lowers waste, and gives developers a live view of what is actually happening after handover.

## What this is
This is not just an app, dashboard, or chatbot.
It is a *home energy operating layer* that combines:

- build context
- device context
- homeowner behaviour memory
- energy diagnostics
- proactive nudges
- aftercare routing
- developer visibility
- future smart meter and battery integration

The key idea is that a high-performance home still needs a high-context operator.

## What the product should do by default
The assistant should standardise on three questions for every energy issue:

- **Money:** what is it costing?
- **Comfort:** how is it affecting the lived experience?
- **Risk:** is this just waste, or a sign of a deeper problem?

That should be the default frame for every reply, alert, and report.

## Core product loops

### 1. Diagnose
Turn raw signals into a useful diagnosis.

Examples:
- bill is high
- heat pump is cycling unusually
- EV charging is happening at the expensive time
- solar self-consumption is too low
- the house looks like it is losing heat too quickly

### 2. Educate
Teach the occupant how to use the home properly.

Examples:
- how to live with an A-rated / low-carbon home
- what the heat pump is doing
- what the tariff means
- when to charge the EV
- why a bill moved
- how to avoid wasting energy through behaviour

### 3. Remember
Build household memory over time.

The system should remember:
- heating preferences
- comfort complaints
- maintenance history
- previous fixes
- EV charging habits
- recurring issues
- what the homeowner already tried

### 4. Intervene
Nudge before waste becomes expensive.

Examples:
- "Your EV charging could be cheaper later tonight."
- "Heating demand is unusually high compared with the weather."
- "Solar output is below the level we would expect today."
- "You may be cooling the house the expensive way."

### 5. Escalate
When the issue is not education, route it.

Possible actions:
- do nothing
- change a setting
- upload a bill
- contact the installer
- open a warranty issue
- schedule a check
- produce a developer report

## Why this matters now
The energy transition is making homes more complex, not less.

Homes now contain combinations of:
- heat pumps
- solar PV
- batteries
- EV chargers
- MVHR / ventilation
- smart meters
- tariff optimisation
- better insulation and airtightness

People are being asked to live in houses that behave differently from older stock, but they are rarely taught how to operate them.

That makes OpenHouse valuable as a translation layer between:
- the physical house
- the occupant
- the developer
- the installer
- the energy system

## The wedge
The cleanest first wedge is still **developer-provisioned new builds**.

Why:
- the developer can supply the build data up front
- the product starts with full context instead of reconstruction later
- homeowner handover becomes a product surface
- aftercare gets better
- the scheme gets a data layer no generic portal can match

This is the strongest source of truth for the energy model.

## The wider opportunity
Once the home-level layer works, the model can expand into:

- retrofit homes
- smart-meter connected homes
- housing estates
- communal batteries
- grid coordination
- developer portfolio intelligence
- national benchmarking and energy literacy

That expansion is what makes the opportunity feel large rather than merely useful.

## What makes it defensible
The moat is not just the AI.
It is the combination of:

1. build-time data
2. device metadata
3. usage history
4. human preferences
5. issue history
6. scheme-level aggregation
7. longitudinal performance data

Generic energy tools will have one or two of these.
OpenHouse can eventually have all of them.

## Developer value
Developers pay because this can help them:

- improve handover quality
- reduce aftercare friction
- reduce avoidable callbacks
- prove the scheme is performing properly
- give better buyer education
- produce ESG / HPI-style evidence
- understand where schemes are confusing occupants

## Homeowner value
Homeowners care because this can help them:

- lower bills
- improve comfort
- understand the house
- avoid mistakes
- trust the systems they were given
- know when to escalate something

## Investor value
Investors will like it if it is framed as:

- a growing network of homes
- recurring software revenue
- strong data moat
- energy tailwinds
- housing tailwinds
- a real-world context layer, not a generic AI wrapper

## Open questions
- How much of the initial data must come from the developer?
- Which data sources are enough for version 1?
- What is the minimum alert set that feels magical?
- Which homeowner actions should be automated vs only suggested?
- How do we keep the product simple while the vision expands?

## Positioning line
OpenHouse teaches people how to live in better homes, and gives developers the context to prove those homes are actually performing after handover.
