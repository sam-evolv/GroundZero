# Someone still has to pay

I had a meeting with Enterprise Ireland this week.

My own report was that it went okay. The useful part was much simpler than any funding narrative I could build around it: external commercial validation matters, and the clearest version of that would be a property developer paying for OpenHouse.

If that happens, I may have a credible route to funding.

That is not a funding offer. It is not a written commitment from Enterprise Ireland. It does not mean OpenHouse has been endorsed or that the hard part is over. It means the gate is now harder to misunderstand.

Someone still has to pay.

I have spent months building the evidence that the product exists. OpenHouse is not a slide deck. It has been used in real developments. The live portal was available this week. There is a developer proof pack, an outreach system and a pipeline of accounts and drafts.

But the commercial fact did not change. The Ground Zero record contains no new paid developer validation this week.

The technical record did not move much either. OpenHouse's production source stayed on the same recorded commit. One new preview was ready and another failed, but neither was production and their exact source was not established. The tenant-isolation audit remained blocked because the scheduled checks did not have a safe database-authority path. The live portal returning a successful response proved availability, not homeowner experience, database correctness or commercial traction.

This is where technical specificity can become emotionally convenient. There is always another environment to reconcile, another preview to inspect and another boundary to document. Those things matter, especially when homeowners and developer data are involved. They are still not the answer Enterprise Ireland gave me.

On Friday I paused OpenHouse for the rest of the day and focused on Donworth AI Solutions. The local repository history records a consulting site launched this week, followed by revisions to the offer, company name and contact route. A separate local project records a substantial interactive showcase built across Friday night and Saturday, including a coffee-ordering experience, a multi-branch operations exhibit and a homeware storefront.

That is real work. The showcase also still has uncommitted changes and assets in its working tree. I cannot honestly call it a finished, published proof from the evidence I reviewed. The consulting site and showcase do not become revenue because they look credible or because they were built quickly.

There are two possible readings of that change in focus.

One is that it is a sensible route to cash. Paid consulting work helped fund OpenHouse in the first place. A clear offer and strong live demonstrations could turn the capability I already have into shorter-cycle income while OpenHouse pursues a slower enterprise sale.

The other is that a new site and a beautiful showcase can give me the clean satisfaction that enterprise selling does not. They respond immediately. A buyer does not.

I do not know from the record which interpretation is true. It may be both. That needs my review, not an agent's conclusion.

The same distinction appears elsewhere in the week.

The Cork café and retail app opportunity accumulated a series of careful briefs: customer identity, live menu availability, pickup promises, accessibility, owner reporting, replenishment reminders, privacy declarations and staffing pressure. The work drew useful boundaries around what should be proved and what should stay out of the launch path.

The client engagement itself remained at discovery pending. No secure commerce access had been inspected. No representative order or checkout path had been exercised. No exact budget, signed scope, support agreement or acceptance owner was recorded.

Seven days of better questions did not become one day of client proof.

Aire showed a different version of the same problem. The underlying Hermes project moved quickly throughout the week. The accepted Aire candidate did not. Its verified boundary remained startup only, with the newer compatibility work still local and unaccepted. By Sunday, the local Aire service route was offline, while the upstream engine continued to change underneath it.

That is not failure, but it is not product progress either. Watching an upstream project advance can create a lot of operational motion without completing one useful job for me on my own phone.

The week's most honest pattern is not that nothing happened. A meeting clarified the commercial gate. A consulting surface was launched. A richer showcase was built locally. The Here’s Health risks were mapped more carefully. The agent infrastructure kept recording changes and contradictions.

The pattern is that nearly all of it still sits before payment, acceptance or completed use.

There is a slightly uncomfortable symmetry here. The business has plenty of technical receipts and too few commercial ones. The journal has plenty of business receipts and too few human ones.

Neither gap is solved by writing more confidently.

The customer question is now explicit. So is the personal one.

Did somebody move closer to paying?

And did I record enough of my own week to know what this pace is actually costing?

The answer to the first is not yet, based on the vault. The answer to the second is no.

That is not a heroic ending. It is a useful place to start Sunday.
