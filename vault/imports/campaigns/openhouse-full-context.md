# OpenHouse AI: Full Context and Strategy

A single place that holds the whole picture: what OpenHouse is, why it exists, what is built and proven today, what is coming, the market and regulatory ground it stands on, and where it can go. Written as working notes, not a pitch. Figures that would go in front of anyone external are flagged for sourcing.

---

## 1. The one paragraph version

OpenHouse is an AI layer that sits over a new home and the development it belongs to. For the homeowner it is a brain in every house, trained on that home's own documents and systems, that answers anything about the property and runs it better after handover. For the developer it is a dashboard that turns the same intelligence into a compliance and defect-liability spine, a portfolio view of which homes are drifting, and proof for boards, assessors and solicitors that the homes were built and handed over correctly. One brain, many skins. The wedge is liability and the performance gap in modern low-energy homes. The founder is a working residential developer building the tool for developers, which is the central credibility asset.

## 3. The core thesis

Two forces meet, and OpenHouse sits in the gap between them.

The performance gap. Modern homes are built to high energy ratings with heat pumps, mechanical ventilation with heat recovery, solar and increasingly EV charging. These systems only deliver their promised performance if they are operated correctly, and most homeowners have never been taught how. A heat pump run like a gas boiler short-cycles and burns far more electricity for the same heat. Ventilation switched off makes an airtight house feel cold and stuffy. The result is high bills, callbacks and disappointed buyers in homes that on paper are excellent. The information needed to close that gap exists, but it is buried in manuals nobody reads.

The liability spine. Regulation is moving hard toward making developers carry the long tail of defects and performance. Ireland has a multi-billion euro apartment defects redress scheme. The UK Building Safety Act 2022 attaches criminal liability and long look-back periods. In that world, the developer who can prove, per home, that everything was certified, commissioned, handed over and is operating correctly, is in a structurally better position than one who cannot. Documentation upkeep stops being admin and becomes risk management.

OpenHouse turns the same per-home intelligence to both jobs at once. It teaches the homeowner to run the house, which closes the performance gap and cuts callbacks, and in doing so it generates and organises the evidence the developer needs for the liability spine. The homeowner gets a better home. The developer gets fewer callbacks and a defensible record. The same brain serves both.

Why now. The regulatory tailwinds are recent and sharpening, the systems creating the performance gap are now standard in new build, and AI has only just become good enough to reason over a specific home's documents and data and give a homeowner a genuinely useful, grounded answer rather than a generic one.

## 4. The product suite

OpenHouse is one intelligence presented through several surfaces. Internally this is "one brain, many skins", branded as OpenHouse Intelligence where it appears.

### Property Assistant and Homeowner Portal
An AI chat assistant trained on a specific property's documents, reached by the homeowner after handover through a QR code or a unit code. It answers questions about the home, the development and the local area, grounded in that home's own data. This is the surface a buyer touches every day and the front door to the homeowner relationship.

### Developer Dashboard
The developer-facing product: sales pipeline, kitchen selections, compliance tracking, scheme analytics, and OpenHouse Intelligence embedded as an assistant. This is where the liability spine lives, where a developer sees a portfolio of homes and where the homeowner-side signals surface as developer-side insight.

### OpenHouse Intelligence
The assistant layer that appears across the dashboard and portal contexts. One model, reskinned to its context, answering a homeowner's bill question on one screen and a developer's portfolio question on another, off the same underlying data.

### OpenHouse Agent
A voice-first AI assistant for Irish sales and letting agents: drafting emails, managing viewings, running applicant workflows. Letspaces, with bulk email, a view-viewing voice capture built out. A note on positioning: do not pitch it as a new-homes developer tool and a second-hand lettings tool at the same time, because the data shape already leans new-homes.

### OpenHouse Care
An aftercare portal concept for renewable energy installers, heat pumps and solar, built on a flywheel: the installer gets a free portal in exchange for introductions to developers. There is no live installer client at present. SE Systems Cork is dead and must never be referenced as a proof point.

### OpenHouse Select
A premium concierge tier for bespoke and one-off builders, the kind who deliver a small number of high-specification homes and want a white-glove version of the homeowner experience. Named prospects in this space include Sigma Homes, KMC Homes and Murphy New Homes.

### The energy-assistant expansion (current direction)
The homeowner assistant is being extended into an omnipresent home energy assistant with full cross-device context, the heat pump, solar, EV charger and ventilation, auto-provisioned at handover. The homeowner asks why a bill is high and the assistant reasons across every system at once and tells them the cause and the fix. This is the direction proven in the Cairn work below, and it is the strongest single expression of the homeowner-side value.

## 5. The compliance and defect spine (the commercial wedge)

The homeowner assistant is the wonder. The compliance and defect spine is what a developer pays for.

The category is proven and the white space is real. Clixifix, a pre-AI defect-logging tool, raised roughly one million dollars and sold for roughly thirteen million. It validated that developers will pay to log and manage defects, and it did so without any of the reasoning, grounding or homeowner-facing intelligence that OpenHouse brings. The space above a simple logging tool is open.

The sales psychology is loss aversion, framed through the Defects Bill and the liability regime. The pitch is not "here is a nice feature", it is "here is how you avoid the cost and exposure that is coming". The compliance screen is the hero asset for developer demos, because it is the thing a developer can hand to a board or a solicitor: a per-home record of what is certified, commissioned, handed over and operating. The most persuasive single artifact is a clean compliance PDF export, a physical document the developer can produce on demand.

There is a Home Performance Index angle. OpenHouse contributes to roughly ten percent of an HPI v3.1 score, across Consumer Information and Aftercare, Post Occupancy Evaluation and supporting indicators. For developers who care about HPI, OpenHouse moves a real and measurable part of the score.

## 7. The Cairn demo (today's work, full record)

Today was a strategy and live engineering session to prepare OpenHouse for a Monday afternoon call with Cairn Homes, Ireland's largest housebuilder and the warmest lead to date. Ollie O'Brien, a Technical Manager at Cairn, is the champion who flagged Sam in. Jennifer O'Neill owns the portal decision and is the evaluator. Jen has noted that Cairn already runs a customer portal, which they are moving to a new platform, and that they are trialling Matterport, which she thinks is a similar solution.

### The strategy
Do not pitch a homeowner portal. They have one and are replacing it, so leading with a portal walks straight into a comparison they are already running. Anchor instead on the compliance and defect-liability spine and on the performance gap, the heat pump and ventilation misoperation that Matterport and a document portal cannot touch.

The framing is three layers of where a home's value sits. Sell, which is Matterport, the home before you buy. Buy, which is their portal. Live, which is what OpenHouse owns, the home after you move in, plus the compliance spine that protects the developer while the homeowner lives there. The lines that carry it: "a portal is a place, Matterport is a picture, OpenHouse is a brain in every home", and "Matterport shows the home before you buy, OpenHouse runs the home after you move in and protects Cairn while it does".

All demo data is synthetic and openly flagged as not yet live but nearly there. The honesty line is part of the script, not a thing to hide.

### What was built and proven
A fully isolated Cairn demo tenant and one richly populated demo home, 34 Bayly, Douglas, Co. Cork, an A2-rated three-bed semi with an air-source heat pump, solar, an EV charger and heat-recovery ventilation. The showcase month is set to January so a winter bill question is intuitive even in a June meeting. The home carries a coherent twelve-month energy history plus its systems, meter details and a bin-collection schedule.

The centrepiece is the bill diagnosis. The homeowner types "why is my electricity bill so high this month", and the assistant reasons across every system at once and answers with the real numbers: the heat pump short-cycling and using about forty-five percent more electricity, roughly a hundred and twenty-eight units more than it should for the same heat; the EV left in fast mode drawing on the day rate, about thirty-two euro recoverable by moving to the night rate; the lowest solar month of the year at ninety-five units with no battery so most of it exported; and a tariff whose import landed in the dearest windows. Loss, cause and a quantified action on every line, ending in an offer to help change the setting. No scripted bot produces that stack of reasons, and that is the moment that makes a technical evaluator sit up.

This was verified live, end to end, on a preview deployment:

- The solar question returns the exact seeded figure, ninety-five units, named as the lowest month of the year.
- The bill question returns the full layered diagnosis off the real numbers.
- The cold-house question names the heat pump, reads the flow-temperature setpoint, and points at the ventilation and drafts, all grounded.
- The BER question returns A2.
- The bins question returns the exact seeded collection schedule, general fortnightly on Tuesdays, organic weekly, recycling alternating, confirmed as real data not invented.
- The photo snag runs the image-triage path: a photo of a hairline crack is identified, contextualised as settlement over the first twelve to eighteen months, triaged honestly as cosmetic with the escalation signal named (widening past a couple of millimetres, or water staining), and offered up to be logged for the site team, without pretending to a structural verdict from a photo.
- Logging it created a real, structured record in the database against this exact home: category cosmetic, severity low, safety risk false, status homeowner_new, scoped to the Cairn tenant, the Bayly development and 34 Bayly, and linked back to the photo analysis. Photo to triage to logged defect, the full loop, persisted.

That logged record is also the bridge to the developer side. The snag a homeowner logs from their sofa is the same row that surfaces in the developer's issue list and the "which homes will generate a callback" view. The "one brain, both sides" claim is therefore backed by a real record flowing from homeowner to developer, not a slide.

### The technical story (worth keeping, because the diagnosis was the hard part)
The energy reasoning was first built into the wrong route. The work was added to the legacy RAG chat route, but the homeowner app actually answers through a newer Assistant V2 endpoint that resolves home context one way and answers on a different model. The symptom was telling: the assistant knew the address but had none of the energy data and was not on the premium model. The address and the energy were coupled in the route that was edited and decoupled in the route the app actually calls, which is exactly the pattern that showed on screen.

The fix was small and clean once located. The Assistant V2 path already serialises a "this home" context object into the model. So the energy dataset was surfaced into that context object at its loader, which means it now reaches the model with no new injection code, and the strong general model reasons over it well enough to produce the layered answer on its own, with no prompt changes needed. The seed, the data, the environment and the premium-model gate were all fine. The only error was which route the change first landed in, and that is now resolved and verified.

A separate note on the model. A premium-model gate was run and passed earlier in the session, confirming the strongest available reasoning model is correct and live. The homeowner answers shown were produced by the standard strong model with the real data in context, and they already hit the layered shape, so the premium model is a polish lever rather than a requirement for Monday.

### State after today
The homeowner half of the Cairn demo is built, live on the preview, grounded in the seeded home, verified end to end across chat and image triage, and the runbook script matches what the assistant actually says. The remaining pieces are a device tab that visualises the systems the assistant reads from, and the developer-dashboard half of the demo, neither of which was needed to harden the half that carries the wonder.

## 8. Technical architecture

Stack. A Next.js monorepo on Vercel, Supabase for the database, OpenAI for the models, and Capacitor wrapping the homeowner web app as an iOS app.

The two-route reality, which is the single most important architectural fact to remember. The homeowner assistant has more than one endpoint. There is a legacy RAG chat route, and there is an Assistant V2 multimodal route that the homeowner app actually calls for both text and images. The V2 route resolves a home's context through a house-context loader and answers through an agent service. Any future change to how the homeowner assistant reasons must go into the V2 path, because that is the one the app uses. This is exactly the thing that cost time today.

House context. The V2 agent receives a serialised "this home" object built by a loader that runs service-role queries for the unit, its rooms, scheme facts and now its energy dataset. Anything added to that object reaches the model automatically, which is the clean injection point for new home data.

The demo tenant. A fully isolated, additive, reversible Cairn demo tenant and home were seeded directly, separate from real homeowner data, with the home's full energy dataset stored on the unit and four connected systems. It can be torn down cleanly by its identifiers.

Design system. Dark canvas with gold-wash gradients, gold accent, charcoal and ink, the Inter typeface, Lucide icons only, a 1280px container. No em dashes anywhere, no emoji in the UI.

iOS. The homeowner app is a Capacitor-wrapped progressive web app, submitted to the App Store and awaiting review. Outstanding before the next submission: run a Capacitor sync and add the Push Notifications capability in Xcode.

Marketing site. Rebuilt on Vercel around a four-product structure, Property Assistant, Developer Dashboard, Intelligence and Care, with Agent removed from the public navigation.

## 9. Market and regulatory context

The Irish market is fragmented, which shapes the entire go-to-market. Around ninety percent of roughly nine hundred Irish builders deliver fewer than fifty units a year. The optimal first customer tier is the mid-size developer delivering somewhere between eighty and four hundred units a year, big enough to feel the pain and pay for the cure, small enough to move quickly.

The regulatory tailwinds are the reason the liability spine sells. Ireland's apartment defects redress scheme runs into the low billions of euro. The UK Building Safety Act 2022 brings criminal liability and a multi-billion pound cladding remediation estimate. The vast majority of new Irish homes are now on heat pumps, with a documented and widespread misoperation gap, which is precisely the performance gap OpenHouse closes.

The incumbent benchmark is Clixifix, which validated the defect-logging category and exited modestly as a pre-AI tool, leaving the intelligent, homeowner-connected version of the category open.

A note on figures, per Sam's rule that statistics only appear when properly sourced. Every number in this section, the share of small builders, the scale of the Irish redress scheme, the UK cladding estimate, the heat-pump adoption figure, and the Clixifix numbers, is a working figure that needs a proper footnoted source before it goes into anything external. They are directionally right and useful for thinking, but they are not yet citation-grade.

## 10. Commercial model

Pricing conviction: twenty-five thousand euro per development per year, on a twelve-month term, no free pilots. Free pilots undermine credibility and signal that the product is not worth paying for, so they are off the table. The price is anchored to the liability and callback cost it removes, not to a per-seat software comparison.

The customer tiers, in order of fit: mid-size developers, eighty to four hundred units a year, as the optimal first tier; larger housebuilders such as Cairn as the marquee accounts that prove scale; and bespoke one-off builders served through OpenHouse Select as a premium concierge tier.

The near-term goal is singular: land the first paying developer customer. Cairn, via Ollie and the Home Performance Index angle, is the warmest named lead.

## 12. Where this goes

The homeowner assistant becomes an omnipresent home brain. Not a chat box a buyer opens once, but a guardian that watches the home's systems, notices the heat pump short-cycling or the ventilation switched off, and reaches out before it becomes a bill or a callback. The proactive notification, the home contacting the homeowner, reframes the whole product from a tool you open to a guardian that watches, and it is cheap to build relative to the shift in perception it creates. The Cairn work proved the reasoning that makes this possible.

The developer side becomes predictive. The same intelligence that answers a homeowner's question rolls up into a portfolio view that ranks which homes are most likely to generate a callback this month, off real per-home signals, and offers to draft the proactive note. The back catalogue is where the liability sits, the homes already handed over and no longer watched, and surfacing those is a large part of the value.

UK entry is planned for year two or three. The architecture is already globally scalable, any jurisdiction's regulatory corpus can be loaded, so the constraint is go-to-market, not engineering. Worth tracking for UK data-model compatibility are Moverly and the PDTF standard, so OpenHouse speaks the UK's emerging property-data language when it arrives.

The Care flywheel turns installers into a distribution channel. A renewable-energy installer gets a free aftercare portal in exchange for introductions to the developers they already work with, which seeds new developer relationships at low cost. It needs a first live installer client to start turning.

A HomeBond strategic partnership is pinned for later sequencing, as a route to embed OpenHouse in the warranty and aftercare flow at scale.

The data moat compounds. Every live deployment's query logs are a proprietary dataset, what real homeowners in real new-build homes actually ask and struggle with, which both sharpens the product and is itself the kind of proprietary insight that opens doors, including the NDRC introduction.

The end state is OpenHouse as the intelligence layer over the entire life of a new home, from the developer's compliance and handover, through the homeowner's daily running of the house, to the warranty and aftercare tail, with one brain serving every party and the developer's liability and the homeowner's experience improved by the same system.

## 13. Principles and conventions

How Sam works, and how anyone or anything working with him should operate.

Copy and style, treated as hard rules. No em dashes anywhere, in code, copy, commit messages or homeowner messages. No emoji in product UI. No corporate or AI-sounding language. Irish and British spelling throughout. An understated, peer-to-peer Irish tone. Statistics only when properly sourced with footnotes. Lucide icons only in the UI.

Engineering workflow. One commit per task, then stop. One component per coding session, to avoid timeouts. A pre-merge diff gate, confirm exactly which files are in a commit before merging. Atomic squash commits with conventional-commit titles. Never write to production without explicit approval. Diagnose thoroughly before acting, and flag blockers clearly while still making progress on whatever is unblocked. For the database: run diagnostics before any data change, take backups before schema changes, and keep schema changes, access rules and data changes as separate steps.

Working relationship. Complementary and collaborative, not condescending. Push back once when warranted, then execute. Prefer concrete named outputs over options menus when the right answer is knowable. Keep momentum.
